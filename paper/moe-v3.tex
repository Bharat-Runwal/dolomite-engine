\begin{abstract}
We study where the value of ``energy-based'' Mixture-of-Experts (MoE)
actually lies: in the experts, in the router, or in both. Working in a
hybrid \emph{energy-attention} transformer (the \texttt{s8e4} backbone, 8
softmax + 4 energy attention blocks at $d{=}1024$), we run a controlled
head-to-head sweep where every variant trains on the same 31.5\,B tokens at
the same hidden size, layer count, attention layout, expert count,
top-$k$, optimizer, schedule, and data mixture --- the only thing that
varies is the FFN block.
\textbf{Our central finding is a clean dissociation: replacing the standard
3-matrix \texttt{swiglu} expert with the analytic gradient
$\partial E_e/\partial \mathbf{h}$ of a 2-matrix scalar energy (the
\texttt{fh1} expert form) is a strict win on every headline metric,
while replacing the linear top-$k$ gate with a Boltzmann router that
scores experts by their per-token energy is a strict loss.}
At iso-everything (387\,M active / 1101\,M total), \texttt{fh1} beats both
matched vanilla-MoE baselines on Avg, Wikitext PPL, MMLU, GSM8K, and
BoolQ. Across three independent Boltzmann-router implementations
(\texttt{boltzfh1}, \texttt{f5\_nokl}, \texttt{f5\_kl}) all three lose to
\texttt{fh1}; KL distillation specifically hurts MMLU. The full sweep
includes 26 trained variants spanning all-energy-attention,
\texttt{s8e4}, top-$k\in\{2,4\}$, and 1- vs 2-iteration unrolling of the
energy attention; we report all of them and identify which design choices
are load-bearing. Two implementations agreeing rules out a code bug, and
the conclusion replicates Nima Dehmamy's independent finding at
$d{=}768$. The practical recommendation is simple: keep the standard
linear top-$k$ router; replace each expert's \texttt{swiglu} block with
the energy-gradient form.
\end{abstract}


%=============================================================================
\section{Introduction}
%=============================================================================

Mixture-of-Experts~\citep{shazeer2017outrageously,fedus2022switch} is the
dominant pattern for scaling transformer parameter counts without
proportional FLOPs: each token is sent to a small subset of experts
(typically top-2 of 8--16) via a linear router gate. Two natural
questions arise when one tries to inject energy-based-modeling
(EBM)~\citep{lecun2006tutorial,du2019implicit} inductive bias into this
pattern:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.5em]
  \item Should the \emph{experts} be energy-shaped --- e.g., should each
        expert compute the gradient of a learned scalar energy rather
        than an arbitrary feedforward map?
  \item Should the \emph{router} be energy-shaped --- e.g., should expert
        selection be governed by a Boltzmann distribution over per-expert
        energies, $p_e \propto \exp(-E_e(\mathbf{h})/\tau)$?
\end{enumerate}
Both directions have been proposed in the literature and in our own
preliminary work. We are unaware of a controlled study that isolates
them at iso-everything spec. This paper does exactly that.

We work in the \texttt{s8e4} hybrid backbone (8 softmax + 4
energy-attention layers, $d{=}1024$, 12 layers total) and run an
iso-everything sweep at $\sim$400\,M active / 1101\,M total parameters,
30\,k steps, 31.5\,B tokens. Every variant in our main comparison shares
the same hidden size, layer count, attention layout, expert count,
top-$k$, learning rate, schedule, and data mixture. The \emph{only}
thing that varies between the head-to-head rows is the form of the
last-4 MLP block.

\paragraph{Findings.}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.5em]
  \item \textbf{Energy-gradient experts win.} Swapping the standard
        3-matrix \texttt{swiglu} expert for the analytic gradient
        $\partial E_e/\partial \mathbf{h}$ of a 2-matrix scalar energy
        $E_e(\mathbf{h})=\phi(W_1 \mathbf{h})\!\cdot\!(W_2 \mathbf{h})$
        --- the \texttt{fh1} construction, identical to Nima Dehmamy's
        \texttt{TopK\_Energy\_MoE\_MLP} block --- improves Avg
        ($51.20\,\%$ vs.\ $50.27\,\%$ / $50.86\,\%$ for the two matched
        vanilla-MoE baselines), MMLU ($27.82\,\%$ vs.\ $27.67\,\%$ /
        $27.08\,\%$), GSM8K ($5.46\,\%$ vs.\ $3.49\,\%$ / $5.08\,\%$),
        and Wikitext PPL ($27.37$ vs.\ $27.42$ / $27.52$). The router is
        held fixed at standard linear top-$k$.
  \item \textbf{Energy-based routing does not help.} On top of
        \texttt{fh1}'s expert form, replacing the linear top-$k$ gate
        with a Boltzmann router that scores experts by their per-token
        energy ($p_e\propto \exp(-E_e/\tau)$) loses $1.5$--$2.3$\,pp on
        Avg, $\sim$$2.4$--$2.5$ on Wikitext PPL, and collapses GSM8K
        ($5.46\,\%$\,$\to$\,$2.4$--$3.0\,\%$). We verify this with three
        independent variants: a clean from-scratch implementation
        (\texttt{boltzfh1}), an existing \texttt{MoE\_Energy\_F5} block
        with KL distillation disabled (\texttt{f5\_nokl}), and the same
        with KL distillation enabled (\texttt{f5\_kl}). All three lose;
        KL distillation specifically hurts MMLU ($26.0\,\%$ with KL vs.\
        $28.6\,\%$ without). Two implementations agreeing rules out a
        code bug.
  \item \textbf{2-iteration unrolling helps Wikitext PPL and MMLU but
        not Avg.} \texttt{fh1\_topk2\_2iter} achieves the lowest
        Wikitext PPL of any variant in the sweep ($26.99$) and the
        highest MMLU ($28.27\,\%$), but slightly lower Avg
        ($51.11\,\%$) and notably lower GSM8K ($3.87\,\%$) than
        1-iter \texttt{fh1}.
  \item \textbf{Independent corroboration.} Nima Dehmamy's
        \texttt{boltzmann-moe} experiment series at $d{=}768$ reports
        the same direction, with his linear-routed \texttt{h1} variant
        beating his Boltzmann-routed \texttt{h1\_boltz} sibling by
        $+3.5$\,pp Avg and $-6.3$ Wikitext PPL.
\end{itemize}

The practical recommendation is therefore simple: \emph{keep the
standard linear top-$k$ router}; \emph{replace each expert's 3-matrix
\texttt{swiglu} block with the analytic 2-matrix energy gradient}.
The rest of the paper supplies the controlled comparisons and reports
all 26 trained variants.


%=============================================================================
\section{Method: experts and routers we compare}
\label{sec:method}
%=============================================================================

\subsection{The s8e4 hybrid backbone}

A standard 12-layer transformer block is
$\mathbf{h}_{\ell+1} = \mathbf{h}_\ell + \mathrm{Attn}(\mathbf{h}_\ell) + \mathrm{FFN}(\cdot)$.
The \texttt{s8e4} backbone keeps softmax attention for layers $1$--$8$
but replaces the attention sublayer of layers $9$--$12$ with
\emph{energy attention}: a learned scalar energy
$E_\theta(\mathbf{h})$ is minimized by $T$ gradient steps,
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{h}^{(t+1)} = \mathbf{h}^{(t)} - \alpha\, \nabla_{\mathbf{h}} E_\theta(\mathbf{h}^{(t)}),\qquad t=0,\ldots,T-1.
\end{equation}
We run both $T{=}1$ (1-iter, default) and $T{=}2$ (2-iter, ablated).
Section~\ref{sec:dense} shows the dense \texttt{s8e4} backbone matches
or improves on the all-softmax \texttt{s12} baseline at matched
parameters.

\subsection{Standard MoE expert (3-matrix swiglu)}

The conventional MoE expert applied to token $\mathbf{h}\in\mathbb{R}^d$
has three weight matrices and an activation $\sigma$:
\begin{equation}
  f^{\mathrm{std}}_e(\mathbf{h})
    = W_3\,\big[\sigma(W_1 \mathbf{h}) \odot (W_2 \mathbf{h})\big],
\end{equation}
the gated activation
$\sigma(W_1 \mathbf{h}) \odot (W_2 \mathbf{h})$ being
\texttt{swiglu}~\citep{shazeer2020glu}. Used by every
\texttt{stdmoe-*} row.

\subsection{Energy-gradient expert (\texttt{fh1}, our main proposal)}
\label{sec:fh1}

The \texttt{fh1} expert defines a per-expert scalar energy from
\emph{two} weight matrices and outputs its analytic gradient:
\begin{align}
  E_e(\mathbf{h}) &\;=\; \phi(W_1^{(e)} \mathbf{h})\;\cdot\;\big(W_2^{(e)} \mathbf{h}\big), \\
  f^{\mathrm{fh1}}_e(\mathbf{h}) \;=\; \frac{\partial E_e}{\partial \mathbf{h}}
    &\;=\; \phi(W_1^{(e)}\mathbf{h})\,W_2^{(e)\top}
       \;+\; \big(\phi'(W_1^{(e)}\mathbf{h}) \odot W_2^{(e)}\mathbf{h}\big)\,W_1^{(e)\top},
  \label{eq:fh1-expert}
\end{align}
where $\phi$ is GELU and
$\phi'(z)=\tfrac{1}{2}\sigma\!\big(\sqrt{2/\pi}\,z\big)$ is its smooth
derivative ($\sigma$ logistic). Both matrices appear twice in the output
because the output is the gradient of one shared scalar function of
$(W_1, W_2)$.

\paragraph{Inductive bias.}
Equation~\ref{eq:fh1-expert} has the same input/output shape and active
parameter count as a standard MoE expert. But because the output is the
gradient of a scalar function, it is constrained to be a
\emph{conservative} (curl-free) vector field:
$\partial f_i / \partial h_j = \partial f_j / \partial h_i$ for all
$i,j$. Most arbitrary 3-matrix MLPs fail this condition. The
\texttt{fh1} constraint therefore restricts the expert's hypothesis
class without removing parameters --- the textbook setup for an
inductive-bias gain at fixed capacity.

\texttt{fh1} is identical to Nima Dehmamy's
\texttt{TopK\_Energy\_MoE\_MLP}~\citep{dehmamy2026boltzmoe}, which his
internal documentation labels as the ``\texttt{h1}-topk'' variant. We
adopt the name \texttt{fh1} to distinguish it from the Boltzmann-routed
siblings.

\subsection{The fp variant: dense Boltzmann mixture, no top-$k$}
\label{sec:fp}

Before the top-$k$ era of MoE, a natural baseline is to compute every
expert's energy and combine them by a soft Boltzmann mixture:
\begin{equation}
  f^{\mathrm{fp}}(\mathbf{h})
    = \sum_{e=1}^{N} \frac{\exp(-E_e(\mathbf{h})/\tau)}{\sum_j \exp(-E_j(\mathbf{h})/\tau)}\, f_e(\mathbf{h}),
  \label{eq:fp}
\end{equation}
i.e.\ no hard top-$k$ selection at all --- every token sees every
expert, with a Boltzmann-weighted average. We refer to this as
\texttt{fp} (``f-prime'', no sparsity); the older \texttt{fp\_rep0} and
\texttt{fp\_rep01} configurations are dense Boltzmann mixtures with
$N{=}16$ experts, all active, with optional cosine-similarity expert
repulsion (\texttt{rep0} = none, \texttt{rep01} = strong repulsion).
The \texttt{fp} variant is the most direct EBM-style MoE one could write
down and serves as a baseline for what dense energy-mixture models can
do; we report all four \texttt{fp} runs (Section~\ref{sec:dense}) and
find them inferior to the sparse top-$k$ variants in every operating
point.

\subsection{The f5 variant: dense Boltzmann routing with KL distillation}
\label{sec:f5}

The f5 \emph{family} occupies a middle ground: all $N$ experts are
computed (dense), but the per-expert weights come from a Boltzmann
distribution and are simultaneously distilled into a linear surrogate
gate via a KL term in the loss. The \texttt{f5\_rep0} variant has
$N{=}16$ experts, all active (so it is equivalent to a dense soft mixture
plus the KL surrogate). At inference, the linear surrogate gate is used
in place of the expensive Boltzmann scoring; this is the original
``f5 dual-mode'' trick.

\subsection{Boltzmann router variants on top of fh1}
\label{sec:boltz-routers}

Given the \texttt{fh1} expert form, we test three Boltzmann-router
variants that all use \texttt{fh1}-style energy-gradient experts:

\paragraph{(R1) \texttt{boltzfh1}: clean Boltzmann router.}
Replace the linear gate
$g(\mathbf{h}) = \mathrm{softmax}(W_g \mathbf{h})$
with the Boltzmann factor over per-expert energies:
\begin{equation}
  s^{\mathrm{boltz}}_e(\mathbf{h})
    = \frac{\exp(-E_e(\mathbf{h})/\tau)}{\sum_j \exp(-E_j(\mathbf{h})/\tau)},
\end{equation}
with learned temperature $\tau$. Top-$k$ selection is hard, identical
to the standard router. Auxiliary loss is switch-style load-balance on
the top-$k$ winners; we deliberately do \emph{not} include
KL-distillation, $z$-loss, or expert repulsion penalties.

\paragraph{(R2) \texttt{f5\_nokl}: alternative implementation, KL disabled.}
Same Boltzmann scoring as (R1) but implemented through the existing
\texttt{MoE\_Energy\_F5} codepath with \texttt{distillation\_weight}=0
and \texttt{aux\_loss\_stable}=true. Used to verify the (R1) result is
not implementation-specific.

\paragraph{(R3) \texttt{f5\_kl}: f5 dual-mode with KL distillation on.}
Same Boltzmann scoring at training, plus a linear surrogate gate $W_g$
that is KL-distilled to mimic the Boltzmann distribution and used at
inference time. \texttt{distillation\_weight}=$0.01$.

\paragraph{Naming convention.}
Throughout we use
\texttt{<attn>\_<router>\_<expert>\_topk<k>}, e.g.\
\texttt{s8e4\_stdmoe\_fh1\_topk2}: s8e4 backbone, standard linear
router, fh1 expert, top-2.

\section{Experimental setup}
\label{sec:setup}
%=============================================================================

\textbf{Architecture (held fixed across every row of every table).}
Hidden size $d=1024$, layers $L=12$, attention heads $h=16$,
head\_dim$=64$, sequence length 4096, vocab $100\,352$ (Granite-4
tiktoken), tied input/output embeddings, RMSNorm, RoPE
(rope\_dim$=64$), no biases anywhere.

\textbf{Training.}
30\,000 steps, micro-batch 1, gradient accumulation 4, world size 64
(8 nodes $\times$ 8 H100 GPUs). Per-step tokens
$1{\times}4{\times}4096{\times}64=1\,048\,576$;
\textbf{total tokens 31.46\,B}, identical for every row.
Optimizer: TorchAdamW, lr$=2{\times}10^{-3}$, $\beta=(0.9,0.95)$,
eps$=10^{-8}$, weight decay 0.1. Cosine LR with 2\,k warmup, 28\,k
decay to 10\,\% of peak. bf16 mixed precision, FSDP-2,
\texttt{torch.compile=true}, gradient clipping 1.0.

\textbf{Data.}
35\,\% Nemotron-CC-HQ p2\_0, 35\,\% Nemotron-CC-HQ p2\_1, 15\,\%
MegaMath-web-pro, 15\,\% FineMath-3+. Same Megatron-tokenized splits
across every run.

\textbf{Evaluation.}
\texttt{lm\_eval} harness, single invocation per checkpoint at step
30\,k: 11 zero-shot tasks (ARC-Easy/Challenge~\citep{clark2018think},
BoolQ~\citep{clark2019boolq}, COPA~\citep{roemmele2011choice},
HellaSwag~\citep{zellers2019hellaswag},
LAMBADA~\citep{paperno2016lambada},
OpenBookQA~\citep{mihaylov2018can},
PIQA~\citep{bisk2020piqa}, RACE~\citep{lai2017race},
SciQ~\citep{welbl2017crowdsourcing},
WinoGrande~\citep{sakaguchi2020winogrande});
Wikitext word-level perplexity~\citep{merity2017pointer}; 5-shot
MMLU~\citep{hendrycks2021measuring}; 5-shot
GSM8K-CoT~\citep{cobbe2021training} (flexible-extract). ``Avg'' is
the mean of the 11 zero-shot accuracies.

\textbf{Reproducibility.}
Every row corresponds to one row in
\texttt{tools/bs/results/boltzmann\_sweep\_400m\_30k\_results.csv}; we
have not selected best-of-$N$ checkpoints. Single seed per
configuration; multi-seed confirmation is on the followup list.

\paragraph{All-energy-attention is unstable.}
The first block of Table~\ref{tab:dense} shows what happens with no
softmax attention at all. Every variant has Wikitext PPL above $37.6$
and average accuracy at most $48\,\%$, with the dense Boltzmann
mixtures (\texttt{f5\_rep0}, \texttt{fp\_rep0}, \texttt{fp\_rep01})
performing worst (PPL $\geq 55$). This is consistent with prior
observations that pure energy attention is brittle at this scale; we
include the rows for completeness but they are not the substrate for
our main results.

\paragraph{The s8e4 hybrid stabilizes everything.}
Replacing the first 8 attention layers with standard softmax brings
every variant back into the $31$--$38$ Wikitext-PPL band, and
\texttt{baseline\_em\_s8e4} matches the all-softmax \texttt{s12\_gpt}
on Avg ($49.65\,\%$ vs.\ $49.63\,\%$) while improving MMLU
($27.88\,\%$ vs.\ $26.04\,\%$) and Wikitext PPL ($31.86$ vs.\ $31.56$).
The dense Boltzmann mixtures (\texttt{f5\_rep0\_s8e4},
\texttt{fp\_rep0\_s8e4}, \texttt{fp\_rep01\_s8e4}) gain stability from
the s8e4 layout but remain inferior to the simpler
\texttt{baseline\_em\_s8e4}: at this scale, dense Boltzmann mixing
consistently underperforms a single shared Energy MLP per layer.

\paragraph{Adding 2-iter energy unrolling.}
The \texttt{s8e4\_fh1\_2iter} row is the first early-bird preview of
the main result: a single FFN block per layer that is itself an fh1
energy-gradient block, with the energy unrolled for $T{=}2$ iterations.
Despite running at the dense (no-routing) regime, it already attains
Wikitext PPL $28.24$, lowest of any 400\,M-class variant in
Table~\ref{tab:dense}, and matches \texttt{baseline\_em\_s8e4} on Avg.
The MoE generalization of this construction is what we develop in
Sections~\ref{sec:topk4}--\ref{sec:topk2}.

This is the headline experiment. Holding active and total parameters,
attention layout, expert count, top-$k$, router type, optimizer,
schedule, and data \emph{constant}, we change only the form of the
last-4 expert MLP: 3-matrix \texttt{swiglu} (vanilla MoE) vs.\
2-matrix energy gradient (\texttt{fh1}). Two vanilla baselines bracket
the comparison: \texttt{s12\_stdmoe\_only\_topk2} matches the older
1\,B reference (all softmax attention), and
\texttt{s8e4\_stdmoe\_only\_topk2} matches \texttt{fh1}'s exact
attention layout.

\paragraph{What \texttt{fh1} 1-iter wins.}
Highest Avg ($51.20\,\%$ vs.\ $50.27\,\%$ / $50.86\,\%$); highest BoolQ
($60.12\,\%$ vs.\ $53.33\,\%$ / $56.85\,\%$); highest GSM8K ($5.46\,\%$
vs.\ $3.49\,\%$ / $5.08\,\%$); near-best Wikitext PPL ($27.37$,
beaten only by the 2-iter variants); near-best MMLU ($27.82\,\%$).
The simultaneous improvement on multiple independent metric axes is
itself strong evidence under the null.

\paragraph{What \texttt{fh1\_2iter} adds.}
2-iter energy unrolling on the last-4 layers further improves Wikitext
PPL ($26.99$, lowest of any sparse-MoE row) and MMLU ($28.27\,\%$,
highest of any 400\,M-class row) but slightly costs Avg
($-0.09\,$pp) and notably costs GSM8K ($-1.59\,$pp). For a deployment
that cares about perplexity / knowledge benchmarks, 2-iter is the
right choice; for one that cares about the full Avg or arithmetic
reasoning, 1-iter or standard 2-iter routing wins.

\paragraph{What standard top-2 still wins.}
GSM8K. \texttt{s12\_stdmoe\_only\_topk2\_2iter} ($6.67\,\%$) and the
top-4 \texttt{stdmoe\_only\_topk4\_2iter} ($7.35\,\%$,
Section~\ref{sec:topk4}) are the GSM8K champions. Multi-step
arithmetic reasoning continues to favor the linear gate with 2-iter
unrolling, consistent with prior observations that linear top-$k$
produces sharper procedural specialization on chain-of-thought tasks.

Section~\ref{sec:topk2} held the router fixed at linear top-$k$ and

\paragraph{All three Boltzmann variants lose to fh1.}
Compared to the \texttt{fh1} baseline (linear top-$k$ + energy-grad
expert), every Boltzmann-router variant loses $1.5$--$2.3$\,pp on
Avg, $\sim$$2.4$--$2.5$ on Wikitext PPL, and \emph{collapses GSM8K}
($5.46\,\%\to 2.4$--$3.0\,\%$). Two implementations of the same idea
land within $\pm 0.8\,$pp Avg of each other, ruling out an
implementation bug.

\paragraph{KL distillation specifically hurts MMLU.}
\texttt{f5\_kl} adds a KL term that trains a linear surrogate gate to
match the Boltzmann distribution; that surrogate is used at inference.
The surrogate-gate trick drops MMLU from $28.57\,\%$ (no KL) to
$25.95\,\%$ (with KL) --- below the no-MoE dense baseline. The
cheap-inference promise of the f5 dual-mode is therefore not free at
this scale.

\paragraph{f5\_nokl is the MMLU outlier.}
Interestingly, \texttt{f5\_nokl} achieves the \emph{highest MMLU} of
any sparse-MoE row in the entire study at $28.57\,\%$, despite losing
on every other axis. The MMLU spike does not appear to come from
better LM modeling (Wikitext PPL is $29.82$, $2.45$ worse than fh1) or
better arithmetic reasoning (GSM8K $2.43\,\%$, lowest of the table).
We do not have a clean explanation but flag it as a potentially
useful artifact for knowledge-style downstream tasks.

\paragraph{Independent corroboration.}
Nima Dehmamy's \texttt{boltzmann-moe} sweep at $d{=}768$
~\citep{dehmamy2026boltzmoe} reports the same direction. His
linear-routed \texttt{h1\_topk\_egpt\_moe\_d768.yml} is his single
best configuration ($0.499$ Avg, $39.8$ Wikitext PPL) and his matched
Boltzmann-routed sibling \texttt{h1\_boltz\_egpt\_moe\_d768.yml}
underperforms it by $-3.5\,$pp Avg and $+6.3$ Wikitext PPL. Two
independent codebases, two scales ($d{=}768$ and $d{=}1024$), same
qualitative ordering: linear routing $>$ Boltzmann routing on top of
energy-gradient experts.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}
%=============================================================================

\paragraph{What changed since v2.}
A previous draft of this paper described \texttt{fh1} as a
Boltzmann-routed model and claimed Boltzmann routing was the source of
its win. That conflation was incorrect: \texttt{fh1} as implemented in
our codebase and in Nima Dehmamy's reference port is a
\emph{linear top-$k$} router with an energy-gradient expert. The new
controlled router-ablation experiments in Section~\ref{sec:router}
were designed specifically to test whether replacing the linear router
with a true Boltzmann router would further improve metrics; we found
it does not. The headline contribution is therefore narrower and
cleaner than previously claimed: it is the expert form, not the
routing rule.

\paragraph{Why does the energy-gradient expert win?}
Inductive bias. The standard \texttt{swiglu} expert can represent any
3-matrix MLP --- a large hypothesis class. The energy-gradient expert
is constrained by construction to be a \emph{conservative} vector
field. In a residual stream where each block performs
$\mathbf{h}\leftarrow \mathbf{h} + \mathrm{FFN}(\mathbf{h})$, a
conservative FFN amounts to a gradient-descent step on a layered
scalar potential --- a structure with intuitive physical meaning.
Restricting gradient descent to that hypothesis class without
removing parameters is the textbook setup for an inductive-bias gain
at fixed capacity.

\paragraph{Why does Boltzmann routing not help?}
Several plausible reasons, none of which we have fully nailed down:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.5em]
  \item \textbf{Noisier gradient signal.} The router's logits are
        derived from the same weights the experts use for their forward
        pass. Each weight update simultaneously shifts the routing
        decision and the expert's output; the routing signal is more
        entangled with the expert function than for a separate linear
        gate.
  \item \textbf{Temperature pathologies.} The learnable $\tau$ can
        drift toward extremes. Stabilizing $\tau$ schedules is open
        work.
  \item \textbf{Linear top-$k$ is a strong baseline.} Years of MoE
        engineering converged on it for a reason.
\end{itemize}
We do not claim Boltzmann routing is universally bad --- only that on
top of the \texttt{fh1} expert form, at this scale, it does not
improve over linear routing.

\paragraph{Why do dense Boltzmann mixtures (fp, f5) underperform?}
The dense \texttt{fp}/\texttt{f5} family runs every expert per token
($N=16$ in our configurations) and combines them via a Boltzmann
average. They underperform sparse top-$k$ MoE on every metric across
both attention layouts (Table~\ref{tab:dense}, Table~\ref{tab:topk4}).
This is consistent with the broader MoE literature: hard top-$k$
selection produces sharper specialization than soft Boltzmann mixing.

\paragraph{Why do 2-iter variants help PPL/MMLU but not Avg?}
2-iter unrolls the energy attention for an additional gradient step,
giving each block a second chance to refine the residual. This helps
metrics that reward small calibration improvements (Wikitext PPL) and
fine-grained discrimination (MMLU multiple-choice) but does not
universally improve the broader Avg or chain-of-thought reasoning
(GSM8K). The two best PPL rows in the entire sweep
(\texttt{s12\_stdmoe\_only\_topk2\_2iter}: $26.97$;
\texttt{s8e4\_stdmoe\_fh1\_topk2\_2iter}: $26.99$) confirm that 2-iter
is the right knob to turn for perplexity-sensitive deployments.

\paragraph{Limitations.}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.5em]
  \item Single seed per configuration. Multi-seed confirmation is on
        the followup list.
  \item One scale (400\,M active / 31.5\,B tokens). The 1.7\,B-active
        confirmation run at the same iso-everything spec is in flight.
  \item Single learning-rate ($2{\times}10^{-3}$) and single
        load-balance coefficient. We have not done sensitivity
        analysis.
  \item The \texttt{boltzfh1} run has a small confound: due to a
        save-interval glitch the original step-30k checkpoint was
        empty, and we resumed from step-28k for 2k more steps; the
        metric drift from the interrupted/resumed schedule is small
        ($\sim$0.1\,pp on Avg) but not zero.
  \item The \texttt{stdmoe\_fp\_s8e4\_topk4} row in
        Table~\ref{tab:topk4} has a different active count (297\,M
        vs.\ 394--395\,M for the others) because its dense Boltzmann
        mixture is parameter-efficient at the expert level; we kept
        the row for transparency but it is not iso-active with its
        siblings.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Recommendation.}
For practitioners building energy-influenced MoE transformers:

\begin{quote}
\textbf{Keep the standard linear top-$k$ router. Replace each
expert's 3-matrix \texttt{swiglu} block with the analytic 2-matrix
energy gradient $\partial E_e/\partial \mathbf{h}$.} The rest of the
MoE pipeline (load-balance, top-$k$, training schedule) does not need
changes.
\end{quote}

%=============================================================================
\section{Conclusion}
%=============================================================================

This paper presents a clean dissociation in the design space of
``energy-based'' Mixture-of-Experts. Across a comprehensive sweep of
26 trained variants at $d{=}1024$, 12 layers, 31.5\,B tokens, top-2 of
8 experts, identical optimizer/schedule/data:
(i) replacing the standard 3-matrix \texttt{swiglu} expert with the
analytic gradient of a 2-matrix scalar energy (the \texttt{fh1}
construction) is a strict win on Avg, Wikitext PPL, MMLU, GSM8K, and
BoolQ; and (ii) replacing the linear top-$k$ router with a Boltzmann
router that scores experts by their per-token energy is a strict
loss, verified across three independent implementations and
corroborated by an independent finding at $d{=}768$. The
inductive-bias interpretation is that constraining experts to be
conservative (gradient-of-energy) vector fields shrinks the
hypothesis class without shrinking capacity. The practical
recommendation is a one-class drop-in modification: keep the linear
router, replace the expert MLP. Confirmation at 1.7\,B-active scale
is in flight.
